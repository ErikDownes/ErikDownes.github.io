## Scope | What this dashboard is for

This is an **independent portfolio project built from public sources**. It is not an airline, lessor or manufacturer system and does not contain proprietary maintenance records.

The dashboard deliberately focuses on the **ATR family only**: the 42 and 72, including the 500 and 600 generations. It connects fleet context with the utilisation measures that matter in asset management.

## Asset view | What would a lessor want to know?

For an individual ATR, the useful questions are not just “How old is it?” or “How far has it flown?”

A stronger asset record asks:

**MSN → registration → model → engine → lessee / operator → FH → FC → calendar age → next maintenance events → remaining component life → source / records status**

That turns an aircraft list into an asset-management dataset.

## Maintenance | Hours, cycles and calendar time

A flight cycle is one take-off and landing. Short regional sectors can therefore generate high cycle counts relative to flight hours.

ATR has publicly stated that its Type A interval was extended to **750 flight hours** and its Type C interval to **8,000 flight hours** across its aircraft series. Those figures are reference points rather than a substitute for the approved maintenance programme of a particular MSN.

[ATR maintenance interval source →](https://www.atr-aircraft.com/presspost/atr-receives-easa-approval-for-extension-of-type-c-maintenance-visit-intervals-by-60/)

## Data boundary | Do not invent an exact current fleet total

ATR reported **1,700+ deliveries as of December 2024**. Its February 2026 results reported a backlog of **more than 160 aircraft**, 19 new operators during 2025 and more than 90 second-hand transactions during that year.

Those figures describe different populations: delivered aircraft, current operators, transactions and future backlog. They should not be added together or treated as an exact count of aircraft currently in service.
