**Start with what you already know.** You do not need a separate speech for every interview question. Build a small collection of strong answers, understand the different things each answer demonstrates, and learn when to use it.

An interview is a two-way conversation. Your education, work and projects give you something useful to offer. The aim is to explain that evidence clearly, listen carefully and keep getting better. Work changes, especially as technology advances, so learning to explain your contribution is a valuable skill in its own right.

## Which question does not fit?

Here is the exercise: **start with a random answer**, not a random question. Look at five possible interview questions. Four can be answered using this evidence, with a different emphasis; **one is a poor match**. Choose the one that does not fit, then check *why*.

A good answer may fit several questions, but **not every question**. The important skill is selecting relevant evidence, then adapting the emphasis. Do not force an unrelated story into an answer.

## What sort of answer am I giving?

<div class="answer-ways" aria-label="Three types of interview response">
<div class="answer-way"><strong>Past experience · I did</strong><p>**STAR**: Situation, Task, Action, Result. Explain a real experience, what **you did**, and what happened.</p></div>
<div class="answer-way"><strong>Future situation · I would</strong><p>Explain the steps you **would take** in a new situation: check, act appropriately, communicate and review.</p></div>
<div class="answer-way"><strong>Knowledge · I know</strong><p>Explain a concept clearly, perhaps with a short example. You do not need to invent a story about everything.</p></div>
</div>

The second is called a **situational or hypothetical question**, rather than the literal opposite of STAR. A question about something you have already done asks for evidence; a question about what you would do asks for judgement. Both can be assessed against the qualities the employer needs.

## How do I know the answer is strong enough?

Aim for a **clear, natural answer of about 20–30 seconds** for an ordinary question. In a story, show the situation briefly, then concentrate on your **action**, the **result**, and what the experience demonstrates. If asked about communication, emphasise communication. If asked about initiative, emphasise what you noticed and did. You do not need to mention every quality in every answer.

After an attempt, ask: **Did I answer the actual question? Did I give evidence? Did I make my contribution clear?** These checks matter more than memorising perfect wording.

## Where do I need more practice?

You should decide what to work on next. Pick your weakest area, say *why* you find it difficult, and practise that area deliberately. There is no value in polishing answers you already know while avoiding the uncomfortable ones.

## How do I take ownership?

**This site is yours now.** You have already done the learning and the work behind these answers. Change what feels unnatural, replace examples you cannot stand over, add better stories, and reorganise the material as your experience grows. Reflection and improvement should continue after any one interview.

Use [**Edit in Pages CMS**](https://app.pagescms.org/ErikDownes/ErikDownes.github.io/main/file/preparation) or [**edit the original file on GitHub**](https://github.com/ErikDownes/ErikDownes.github.io/edit/main/preparation.md). Sign in with your own GitHub account if asked. You can also use ChatGPT to help you revise, but the words, evidence and decisions must remain yours.
