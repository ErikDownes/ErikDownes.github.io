## Aircraft and Engine Maintenance Schedules

**The short version:** the airline keeps the aircraft airworthy, plans the work and arranges qualified engineers. The lessor monitors the aircraft and the lease. If the lease includes maintenance reserves, the airline pays an additional amount linked to use; after qualifying major work, it can submit the records and seek reimbursement. Think **rainy day provision for agreed major events**, but not the airline's own savings account: reimbursement and any unused balance follow the lease. It is not a fee for the lessor to send mechanics or a substitute for ordinary maintenance. [AerCap describes the airline's maintenance responsibility and reserve reimbursements in its annual report](https://www.aercap.com/investors/shareholder-services/sec-filings/content/0001378789-25-000007/aer-20241231.htm).

## What happens while the aircraft is flying?

| Work | What it looks like | Where and how long? |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Before flights and between flights** | Crew inspections, recorded defects, checks and small repairs. A fault can ground the aircraft until resolved. | Engineers can come to the aircraft at an airport. This is **line maintenance**. |
| **Regular planned visits (often called A checks)** | A package of inspections and routine tasks scheduled by hours, cycles or calendar time. | Often planned overnight or during a short period on the ground at a suitable maintenance station. |
| **Heavy planned visits (often called C checks)** | More extensive inspection, access to systems and structure, repairs and replacement of components. | The aircraft is taken out of service at a **base-maintenance hangar**. Think several days, sometimes longer if the work or findings grow. |
| **Major age-related work** | Larger structural and corrosion inspections, modifications and other tasks due after years in service. | A longer hangar visit may take weeks. The operator may combine these tasks with a C check. |
| **Engine shop visit** | An engine is removed for planned overhaul, repair or an unexpected problem. | A specialist engine shop works on the engine, potentially for much longer than the aircraft's ground time. A serviceable replacement engine can be installed so the aircraft can return to flying. |

These are **illustrations, not a fixed service schedule**. Each aircraft follows its operator's approved maintenance programme, and a flight-hour limit does not cancel calendar or cycle limits. EASA distinguishes line maintenance at an operating station from more extensive base maintenance. [EASA continuing-airworthiness rules](https://www.easa.europa.eu/en/document-library/easy-access-rules/online-publications/easy-access-rules-continuing-airworthiness?erules-id=ERULES-1963177438-238).

## A, B, C and D: are they four compulsory steps?

The letters are familiar shorthand, **not a universal ladder every aircraft climbs in the same way**. A is a lighter package; C is a heavier base visit. A **B check** can mean an intermediate package in a programme that uses one; its tasks may instead be combined with other visits. **D check** commonly describes very extensive, age-related work, but it is not a universal date that every ATR must meet. The actual job list and due dates come from the aircraft's approved programme, and airlines may spread tasks across several visits. [EASA lists A, B and C or equivalent checks](https://www.easa.europa.eu/en/document-library/easy-access-rules/online-publications/easy-access-rules-continuing-airworthiness?erules-id=ERULES-1963177438-677); [Airbus explains how operators group line, base, six-year and twelve-year work](https://www.aircraft.airbus.com/sites/g/files/jlcbta126/files/2022-04/Airbus-FAST55.pdf).

For an **ATR 42 or 72**, ATR announced an A-check interval of **750 flight hours** and a C-check interval of **8,000 flight hours**. Those figures help you picture the scale; they are not a promise that every particular aircraft will always be scheduled at precisely those hours. [ATR on A checks](https://www.atr-aircraft.com/presspost/atr-extends-type-a-maintenance-visit-intervals/) · [ATR on C checks](https://www.atr-aircraft.com/presspost/atr-receives-easa-approval-for-extension-of-type-c-maintenance-visit-intervals-by-60/).

## Picture the first 12 years of one ATR

**Illustrative assumption: 2,000 flight hours a year.** That is about 5½ hours in the air per calendar day on average. It is not an Abelo aircraft schedule.

| Rough point in time | What the hours suggest | What else happens? |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Every operating day** | The aircraft keeps accumulating hours and cycles. | Inspections, defect reports and any needed line maintenance continue. |
| **Roughly every 4–5 months** | Another 750 flight hours have accumulated: an A-check-sized interval. | Other calendar- or cycle-driven tasks may fall due sooner. |
| **Around years 4, 8 and 12** | Each additional 8,000 hours suggests a C-check-sized event. | Work can be grouped differently; discoveries can extend hangar time. |
| **Across the whole 12 years** | Engines, propellers and components have **their own** limits and condition monitoring. | Shop visits and replacements do not have to coincide with a C check. |

The arithmetic is a guide to **planning**, not an approved maintenance programme. If it flew **10 hours on each flying day**, 20,000 hours would take 2,000 flying days. It would receive many inspections and checks during those days. ATR quoted **20,000 hours to overhaul for its newer PW127XT engine**, but that is not a rule for every ATR engine, nor a reason to leave an engine untouched until that point. [ATR's PW127XT announcement](https://www.atr-aircraft.com/presspost/atr-receives-easa-approval-for-extension-of-type-c-maintenance-visit-intervals-by-60/).

## Does an engine shop visit mean it broke down?

**Not necessarily.** Major engine work can be planned because of operating hours, component lives, condition trends or inspections. An unexpected defect can also require an early removal. The airline may take an engine off, fit a serviceable replacement, complete the required checks and fly the aircraft while the removed engine is at the shop. The aircraft must have its required engines installed and cleared for flight; it cannot continue airline service with one removed.

Turboprops and jets follow the same broad pattern of line maintenance, hangar checks and engine work, but the tasks differ. An ATR has a turbine engine **and a propeller system** to inspect and maintain. A jet does not have that propeller system. Engines and propellers can each have their own maintenance events. [ATR describes propeller availability and maintenance in an airline support agreement](https://www.atr-aircraft.com/presspost/emerald-airlines-signs-long-term-global-maintenance-agreement-with-atr/).

## Where does the aircraft go, and what does the lessor do?

For line maintenance, the engineer may come to the aircraft at its usual airport. For a heavy check, the airline can schedule the aircraft to fly to a suitable hangar at its own base **or somewhere else**. It depends on available slots, approvals, aircraft type and the maintenance contract. **Dublin and Shannon are not too small:** both have aircraft maintenance hangars. [Dublin Airport's hangars](https://www.dublinairport.com/b2b/commercial-property/aviation) · [Shannon Airport's maintenance operators](https://snnairportgroup.ie/aviation/aircraft-maintenance-operators/).

**EASA** is the **European Union Aviation Safety Agency**. It helps set the European aviation safety framework; the national authority and approved organisations have their respective oversight and operational roles. [EASA's overview](https://www.easa.europa.eu/en/the-agency/faqs/basic-regulation).

The lessor's asset-management team follows the reported **flight hours, cycles, condition, maintenance records and upcoming major events**. Suppose an eligible engine shop visit is completed: the airline sends its evidence and claim; the lessor checks it against the lease and reimburses the qualifying amount, subject to its terms and balance. That is the connection between the physical maintenance and the [Maintenance Reserves]({{ '/aviation/maintenance-reserves.html' | relative_url }}) page.
