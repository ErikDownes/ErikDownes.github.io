## Asset Management | What is aircraft asset management?

Aircraft asset management is the ongoing management of an aircraft **while it is owned and leased**.

The aircraft is both a physical machine and a financial asset. Its value is affected by age, utilisation, maintenance condition, engine and component life, technical records, market demand and remaining economic life.

**Key idea:** leasing gets the aircraft earning money; asset management helps keep it earning money while protecting its value.

## Ownership Cycle | Where does Asset Management sit?

A useful ownership cycle is:

**Identify opportunity → Acquire → Lease → Manage → Re-lease / sell / convert → End of economic life**

Asset Management sits across that cycle because the same aircraft may change airline, country, configuration or purpose several times while remaining an asset that must be technically, contractually and financially controlled.


## Maintenance Specifications | What actually counts down?

Aircraft maintenance is not measured in **miles** in the way a car is. The key utilisation measures are usually:

- **Flight hours (FH):** time the aircraft spends flying.
- **Flight cycles (FC):** one completed take-off and landing sequence.
- **Calendar time:** some tasks fall due after a stated number of days, months or years even if utilisation is low.
- **Component life:** engines, landing gear, propellers and life-limited parts can each have their own hour, cycle or calendar limits.
- **Condition / configuration:** maintenance status depends on the actual aircraft, engine, component and approved maintenance programme — not just the model name.

**Short-haul point:** two aircraft can accumulate the same flight hours but very different cycle counts. An aircraft flying six one-hour sectors a day accumulates roughly three times as many cycles as one flying two three-hour sectors a day. Take-offs, pressurisation changes, landing loads and repeated engine power changes make cycles particularly important for many fatigue- and life-limited items.

The right language is therefore **hours + cycles + calendar + condition**, not “mileage”.

### ATR maintenance reference

ATR states that, across its aircraft series, the Type A maintenance-check interval was extended from **500 to 750 flight hours**, and the Type C interval from **5,000 to 8,000 flight hours**. These are useful manufacturer-level reference points, but the actual maintenance programme for a particular aircraft is controlled by the approved operator programme, aircraft configuration, service bulletins, airworthiness directives and component limitations.

## Utilisation | Why are cycles so important on regional aircraft?

A **flight cycle is one take-off and landing**. Regional aircraft often fly several short sectors per day, so they can accumulate cycles rapidly even when annual flight hours are moderate.

That matters because repeated ground-air-ground loading contributes to structural fatigue, while take-off and landing also repeatedly exercise landing gear, brakes, propellers and engines through high-power and transient operating conditions.

But cycles are **not the only measure**. Some maintenance tasks are hour-based, some cycle-based, some calendar-based and some condition-based. Good asset reporting therefore keeps FH and FC separate rather than trying to replace both with one “usage” number.

## Maintenance Reserves | Why does maintenance become a financial issue?

Aircraft usage creates future maintenance obligations. Flight hours, flight cycles, engine condition and major maintenance events can therefore affect both cash flow and asset value.

Maintenance reserves are one mechanism used to help fund major future maintenance events and protect the lessor's economic position. Depending on the lease and event, reserve calculations may be linked to **flight hours, flight cycles, calendar time or a defined maintenance event**. The technical counter therefore becomes a financial counter.

This is where technical information becomes a **financial and contractual issue**, not merely an engineering one.

## Systems & Data | What information does an asset manager track?

An aircraft-management system may bring together:

- aircraft and technical data;
- lease dates and contractual obligations;
- utilisation;
- maintenance status and reserves;
- insurance and documentation;
- invoices and billing;
- aircraft values;
- portfolio and management reporting.

**Leasepoint** is useful for seeing the structure of an internal aircraft-management system, while **Cirium** illustrates the wider aircraft-data and valuation environment.

## Cross-Functional Work | Which teams does Asset Management connect?

Asset Management does not operate in isolation.

**Commercial** develops airline opportunities.  
**Finance & Pricing** models cash flows and returns.  
**Risk** assesses airline and market exposure.  
**Technical** understands the aircraft and its maintenance position.  
**Legal** structures the contractual framework.  
**Data & Digital** supports systems, reporting and analysis.

A single aircraft decision can require several of these perspectives at once.

## Decision Support | What should good reporting help people decide?

Good reporting should move from:

**data → pattern → comparison → implication → decision**

Power BI or another reporting system is useful when it helps people see lease obligations, utilisation, maintenance, billing, values or portfolio exposure clearly enough to act.

The important skill is not producing a dashboard for its own sake. It is **turning accurate data into a decision that another person can understand and verify**.
