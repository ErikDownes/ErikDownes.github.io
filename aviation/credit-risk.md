I’ve been thinking about what happens if an airline comes under financial pressure and struggles to make a lease payment. A sharp rise in fuel costs could put pressure on its finances. War, sanctions or political instability could also affect where an aircraft can operate.

I imagine the first step is to talk with the airline, understand the difficulty and see whether there’s a workable solution. The relationship matters, but the lessor also has to protect its financial position and the aircraft.

Security deposits and maintenance requirements provide some protection. The Asset Management team monitors payments, the aircraft’s condition and the relationship with the airline. That helped me see why the day-to-day reporting in this role matters: you want to spot a problem before unpaid amounts grow beyond the protection you have.

If the airline cannot recover, I assume the lessor would consider whether the aircraft could be leased to another operator. Demand for that type of aircraft, its condition and its records would all matter.

**A question I’d like to ask:** How often have you had to re-lease an aircraft because an airline was in financial difficulty? When it happens, is there usually a financial loss, or can the deposit and the terms of a new lease sometimes cover it?

## Terms in plain English

**Credit risk** — The risk that the airline will not pay rent or meet its financial obligations under the lease.

**Security deposit** — Money held under the lease that may help cover obligations if the airline defaults. It is a safeguard, not a guarantee against every loss.

**Maintenance requirements** — The airline’s duties to maintain the aircraft and its records to the standards set out in the lease.

**Re-lease** — Placing an aircraft with a new airline after an earlier lease ends. Its timing and financial outcome depend on the aircraft and the market.
