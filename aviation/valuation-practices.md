## Valuation | What does valuation mean in aircraft leasing?

There are really **two related valuation questions**.

First, there is the **aircraft as an asset**: what is the aircraft worth now, what cash flows can it generate, and what might its residual value be later?

Second, there is the **leasing company as a business**: what is the company worth to an investor given its aircraft, leases, debt, earnings and returns?

That distinction matters. An asset manager may monitor aircraft values and the assumptions that affect them, while equity investors and analysts also value the lessor itself.

## Aircraft Value | How would I think about valuing an aircraft?

I would not expect one number to tell the whole story. I would start with the aircraft type and age, then consider its lease income, remaining lease term, maintenance condition, utilisation, technical records, market demand and expected residual value.

A simple financial model can project the future lease cash flows and an estimated residual value, then discount those future amounts back to today. That is where **NPV — Net Present Value** — becomes useful.

The result is only as good as the assumptions, so I would also use sensitivity analysis and stress testing rather than treating a single valuation as certain.

## Book Value | Is book value the same as market value?

No. **Book value** is an accounting value on the balance sheet. The source material explains that aircraft are brought onto a lessor's balance sheet at cost and then depreciated. That historical accounting path does not necessarily equal the price the aircraft could achieve in today's market.

The AerCap example makes the point very clearly: two hypothetical companies own identical fleets of 100 aircraft, but one paid $40 million per aircraft and the other $60 million. Their book values would therefore be $4 billion and $6 billion even though the underlying fleets are identical.

So a higher book value does not automatically mean a more valuable fleet.

## P/E and P/B | How are aircraft leasing companies valued?

The source describes aircraft leasing as sitting between **industrials** and **financials**. Industrial-company analysis often focuses on **Price-to-Earnings (P/E)**, while financial-company analysis often focuses on **Price-to-Book (P/B)** and **Return on Equity (ROE)**.

Its argument is that P/E can be particularly informative for aircraft leasing because long lease terms, relatively stable aircraft supply and low volatility in asset pricing can create predictability in earnings and cash flows.

The key warning is not to anchor blindly on the same P/B multiple across lessors. Different acquisition prices and different returns can produce very different economics even when the aircraft themselves are identical.

## ROE | Why does Return on Equity matter?

**Return on Equity (ROE)** measures the profit generated relative to shareholders' equity.

In the source's simplified example, the two companies have identical aircraft and both earn $600 million per year. Because Company A acquired the fleet more cheaply, it produces a 15% ROE compared with Company B's 10%.

That is why acquisition discipline matters: what you pay for the asset can affect returns for years afterwards.

## Practical Asset Management | What would an asset manager actually watch?

For an aircraft-level view, I would monitor the factors that can change future cash flow or marketability: lease income, lessee performance, aircraft age, maintenance status, utilisation, records, downtime, transition cost, market demand and residual value.

I would then ask how robust the valuation is. What happens if lease income falls? What if the aircraft takes longer to re-lease? What if maintenance costs rise, the discount rate increases or the residual value is weaker than expected?

That connects valuation directly with **asset management, sensitivity analysis and stress testing** rather than treating valuation as a one-off calculation.

## Key Terms | What terminology should I know?

**NPV — Net Present Value:** the value today of future cash flows after discounting.

**Residual value:** the estimated value of the aircraft at the end of the analysis or lease period.

**Book value:** the accounting carrying value of the asset after depreciation and other accounting adjustments.

**Market value:** an estimate of what the asset could be worth in the current market.

**P/E — Price-to-Earnings:** company market value relative to earnings.

**P/B — Price-to-Book:** company market value relative to book equity.

**ROE — Return on Equity:** profit relative to shareholders' equity.

**Discount rate:** the rate used to convert future cash flows into present value.

**Sensitivity analysis:** changing assumptions to see which variables have the greatest effect on the result.

**Stress testing:** testing deliberately adverse but plausible assumptions to understand downside exposure.
