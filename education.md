## Power-by-the-Hour
<div class="aercap-beamer" data-aercap-beamer data-title="Power-by-the-Hour"><div class="aercap-source">
<p>AerCap normally sets fixed rent at the outset of a lease. During the Covid years, it offered some Power-by-the-Hour, or PBH, contracts in which payments for an initial period depended on the aircraft’s monthly utilisation.</p>
<p>After the PBH period, the aircraft reverted to fixed-rate rental for the rest of the lease. PBH changed the timing of payments while preserving the longer-term fixed-rate contract.</p>
<p>These contracts have always been part of the toolkit. Their usage increased significantly during Covid, peaking at around 140 contracts, or approximately 10%.</p>
<p>Government restrictions repeatedly closed and reopened travel corridors. Airlines expected to use the aircraft over the long term, but could not be certain how much they would fly in the short term.</p>
<p>PBH gave airlines room to cope with that uncertainty. AerCap could protect the later lease rate instead of accepting a low fixed rate for the whole term; as flying recovered, the aircraft generated useful income for both parties.</p>
<p>The cash and accounting pictures are different. Fixed contractual lease revenue is recognised evenly through the contract as straight-line rental; variable PBH receipts are recognised when the aircraft is used.</p>
<p>Take a simplified five-year lease. Year one is PBH; years two to five are fixed at $1 million per year. If the aircraft does not fly in year one, cash is $0 then $1 million in each later year.</p>
<p>The $4 million of fixed rent is recognised at $800,000 per year across all five years, even though no cash arrives in year one. That is the straight-line component.</p>
<p>Now assume the aircraft flies enough to generate $650,000 of PBH rent in year one. That $650,000 is both cash collected and variable revenue recognised in year one.</p>
<p>Accounting revenue in year one becomes $1.45 million: $800,000 straight-line rent plus $650,000 PBH. Years two to five each show $800,000 of accounting revenue, though cash is $1 million each year.</p>
<p>Across the five-year example, total cash and total accounting revenue are both $4.65 million. The difference is timing: cash rises from $650,000 to $1 million, while annual reported revenue falls from $1.45 million to $800,000.</p>
<p>AerCap called the PBH roll-off a headwind to its 2024 revenue guidance as many contracts reverted to fixed rent after late 2023. The accounting comparison changed even as cash collections on those contracts were expected to rise; the underlying contract economics had not worsened.</p>
</div></div>

## Aircraft Leasing 101
<div class="aercap-beamer" data-aercap-beamer data-title="Aircraft Leasing 101"><div class="aercap-source">
<p>Aircraft leasing has been around since the 1970s and has grown in importance and scale ever since. Aircraft lessors today own over 50% of the world’s commercial aircraft, and that share is expected to grow over coming decades.</p>
<p>Airlines use aircraft leasing as a source of financing and also to gain access to new-technology aircraft earlier than they might by going directly to manufacturers.</p>
<p>At the time of this transcript, Airbus and Boeing narrowbody production was sold out until around 2030. A lessor with manufacturer orders could still offer an airline a delivery from its own orderbook in roughly two to three years.</p>
<p>Earlier access to a new aircraft lets an airline benefit from fuel-burn savings sooner and reduces the capital expenditure required to own it outright.</p>
<p>Not all airlines operate in regions with deep capital markets. Leasing is therefore particularly important in countries with strong passenger growth but without the financial infrastructure required to fund large aircraft purchases directly.</p>
<p>At the time, AerCap described serving more than 300 airlines across 80 countries. Its scale links aircraft supply and finance to many different markets.</p>
<p>A common new-aircraft pattern is a twelve-year first lease, followed by two six-year leases or extensions. That carries the aircraft towards a roughly 25-year passenger-service life.</p>
<p>At the end of the first quarter of 2024, AerCap reported an average remaining lease term above seven years. That backlog of contractual rent gave visibility into future income.</p>
<p>AerCap’s leases are almost all fixed rate once the aircraft is delivered. A forward-order lease can allow the lease rate to move between signing and delivery to account for interest-rate and inflation changes during those intervening years.</p>
<p>After delivery, the rent is fixed for the agreed lease term. AerCap funds the aircraft at group level, predominantly with fixed-rate debt; floating-rate debt can be hedged with interest-rate caps and swaps.</p>
<p>Its average remaining debt tenor was approximately six years at the time of the video. Matching long-term leases with long-term diversified funding helps control exposure to changes in interest rates and refinancing conditions.</p>
<p>Combining long-term fixed-rate leases with long-term diversified fixed-rate financing aligns the capital structure and supports consistent returns through the cycle.</p>
</div></div>

## Maintenance Rights Assets
<div class="aercap-beamer" data-aercap-beamer data-title="Maintenance Rights Assets"><div class="aercap-source">
<p>A Maintenance Rights Asset, or MRA, can arise when a lessor buys an aircraft with an existing lease attached, often through M&amp;A.</p>
<p>The new owner acquires a contractual right to receive the aircraft at lease end in better maintenance condition than its condition at the acquisition date. That right has value separate from the physical aircraft.</p>
<p>The concept is particularly relevant to lessors that engage in large-scale M&amp;A because it is a feature of purchase accounting. AerCap encountered substantial MRA after the acquisition of ILFC in 2014 and GECAS in 2021.</p>
<p>Purchase-price accounting separates the acquired asset into two parts on the balance sheet. Flight equipment held for operating lease is the aircraft’s “metal value”; the MRA records the value of the maintenance right in the attached lease.</p>
<p>Although the metal and the Maintenance Rights Asset are both part of the aircraft’s economic value and trade together on sale, they are treated differently for accounting purposes.</p>
<p>The metal value is depreciated on a straight-line basis over the aircraft’s remaining useful life. The MRA is instead amortised when a relevant event occurs over the remaining lease term, such as lease end, aircraft sale or a maintenance shop visit.</p>
<p>Suppose a company acquires a ten-year-old aircraft for $50 million with an existing two-year lease. The redelivery condition in the lease is $15 million better than the aircraft’s actual maintenance condition at acquisition.</p>
<p>Purchase accounting separates the $50 million price into a $35 million flight-equipment asset and a $15 million MRA. The aircraft has about fifteen years of useful life left, but the attached lease has only two years left.</p>
<p>The $35 million of metal value is depreciated over that remaining fifteen-year aircraft life. The $15 million MRA can be amortised when a relevant event occurs within the next two years, creating a much faster expense pattern.</p>
<p>Relative to buying an economically identical aircraft outright, purchase accounting can therefore produce higher effective depreciation, lower reported asset values and lower profits in the earlier years after an acquisition.</p>
</div></div>

## Maintenance Reserves
<div class="aercap-beamer" data-aercap-beamer data-title="Maintenance Reserves"><div class="aercap-source">
<p>Maintenance reserves form a critical part of the cash flows associated with leasing aircraft.</p>
<p>Once an aircraft is delivered, the lessee takes responsibility for staffing, insurance, operations and maintenance. Each lease also specifies the condition in which the aircraft must be returned at the end of the term.</p>
<p>There are two common ways to deal with maintenance condition. The first is supplemental rent, usually called maintenance reserves. The second is end-of-lease, or EOL, compensation for the difference between actual return condition and the condition required by the lease.</p>
<p>A maintenance-reserve payer makes cash payments each month in addition to base rent. The balance accumulates like a sinking fund and helps ensure funds are available for major maintenance events such as shop visits.</p>
<p>Maintenance-reserve payments are usually linked to utilisation, measured by flight hours or flight cycles. The airline whose flying consumes the aircraft’s maintenance life contributes towards the associated future cost.</p>
<p>If an aircraft passes to another operator before the next shop visit, the accumulated reserves help prevent the new airline from bearing all the cost of use by the previous airline.</p>
<p>EOL structures do not require the same monthly reserve payments. At lease end the airline can either perform the maintenance needed to meet the contractual return condition or pay the lessor the agreed shortfall. These arrangements are typically offered to stronger credits.</p>
<p>AerCap describes its reserve accounting as first-in, first-out (FIFO). Reserve cash does not automatically count as profit: expected future reimbursements are recorded as a maintenance liability.</p>
<p>Take a simplified twelve-year lease with a $10 million shop visit expected near the end. The airline pays $1 million per year in reserves, so $12 million is collected over the full lease.</p>
<p>In years one to ten, each $1 million received increases both cash and the maintenance liability. After ten years, the balance sheet therefore shows $10 million of cash and a $10 million obligation for the expected work.</p>
<p>In years eleven and twelve, the extra $2 million collected exceeds the expected $10 million cost. It can be recognised as maintenance revenue in profit or loss and then reflected in equity.</p>
<p>At the end of year twelve, before the work, this simplified example has $12 million cash, $10 million maintenance liability and $2 million equity. Those amounts explain why collecting cash and earning profit are different events.</p>
<p>When the $10 million shop visit occurs, the airline initially pays for the work and claims reimbursement from the lessor. Paying that claim reduces both the lessor’s cash and maintenance liability by $10 million.</p>
<p>Under an EOL contract, cash compensation for an aircraft returned below the stipulated maintenance condition is recorded as maintenance revenue, increasing cash and equity. If the lessor later undertakes the work, the cost is recorded through leasing expenses.</p>
</div></div>

## The Lifecycle of an Aircraft
<div class="aercap-beamer" data-aercap-beamer data-title="The Lifecycle of an Aircraft"><div class="aercap-source">
<p>Stage one is portfolio strategy: deciding which aircraft to own. AerCap seeks in-demand new-technology aircraft with many potential operators and deep orderbooks, so it has multiple placement options.</p>
<p>In the video, the A320neo family, A350, 737 MAX and 787 made up more than 75% of AerCap’s fleet by value. That figure described its portfolio at the time of recording.</p>
<p>Stage two is purchasing. OEM–lessor negotiations can begin three to five years before delivery and cover price, aircraft specification, flexibility, delivery schedules and payment terms.</p>
<p>Large orders require discipline and are relatively infrequent. The transcript cites AerCap’s March 2020 order for fifty A320neo-family aircraft as an example of a major airframe order.</p>
<p>Stage three is leasing. After ordering, the lessor studies local demand, checks an airline’s credit profile and seeks the best credit-adjusted lease opportunity for each aircraft.</p>
<p>Lease terms are often agreed twelve to thirty-six months before delivery. The first lease on a new aircraft is commonly around twelve years and is typically fixed-rate, providing visibility over future cash flows.</p>
<p>Stage four is the mid-life decision. Around years nine to twelve, the lessor considers extending the current lease, finding a new lessee or selling the aircraft.</p>
<p>It makes a relative-value assessment: compare the likely returns from each option in light of the aircraft’s age and condition, leasing demand and market environment.</p>
<p>AerCap generally prefers to sell aircraft with a lease already attached rather than “naked”, because financial buyers value the contracted cash-flow stream.</p>
<p>Stage five is end of life. The cycle of lease, transition and re-lease can repeat several times across an aircraft’s roughly 25-year passenger-service life.</p>
<p>From around year twenty, options include selling the whole aircraft, selling its engines, parting it out or converting it for cargo. The video suggests a cargo conversion can extend service life to roughly 35–40 years.</p>
</div></div>

## Managing Credit Risk in Aircraft Leasing
<div class="aercap-beamer" data-aercap-beamer data-title="Managing Credit Risk in Aircraft Leasing"><div class="aercap-source">
<p>Airlines are cyclical: barriers to entry can be low, passenger demand swings and the marginal cost of filling an otherwise empty seat is small. Investors therefore reasonably ask whether airline default must dominate a lessor’s risk.</p>
<p>AerCap argues that credit costs need not dominate performance for a well-run lessor. Its explanation rests on lease structure, asset quality and active collections.</p>
<p>It puts this down to three things: having the right lease structures, having the right aircraft assets and having the right attitude to collections.</p>
<p>First, long-term fixed-rate leases provide contractual cash flows. New aircraft are often placed on twelve-year leases, with protection through maintenance reserves, security deposits and contractual return conditions.</p>
<p>Second, asset quality gives the lessor alternatives. AerCap argues that an aircraft wanted by many airlines, even if currently leased to a weaker credit, can be preferable to an unpopular aircraft leased to a stronger airline.</p>
<p>If a customer runs into trouble, a widely used aircraft is easier to remarket. AerCap points to the A320neo: demand and lease pricing exist across markets such as the US, Japan and Australia, while an individual airline’s credit can change quickly.</p>
<p>New-technology aircraft such as the A320neo, A350, 737 MAX and 787 have large user bases, order backlogs, more liquid pricing and aftermarket support. Those features broaden the pool of possible replacement operators.</p>
<p>Third, the lessor must collect promptly and monitor the asset. A strong contract still requires oversight of payments, technical condition and an open dialogue with the airline.</p>
<p>As an illustration from the video, AerCap reported receivables averaging only four to five days, against forty to sixty days for truck and equipment lessors. This is a period-specific comparison, not a universal industry rule.</p>
<p>Diversification matters too. AerCap described over 300 airline customers in more than 80 countries, reducing exposure to one operator or region and adding market intelligence about both demand and weakness.</p>
<p>AerCap reported long-run credit costs averaging about 1% of lease revenue. Its conclusion is that lease protections, desirable aircraft, collections and diversification contain the cost; credit risk itself never disappears.</p>
</div></div>

## Valuation Practices in Aircraft Leasing
<div class="aercap-beamer" data-aercap-beamer data-title="Valuation Practices in Aircraft Leasing"><div class="aercap-source">
<p>Aircraft leasing has relatively few publicly listed companies. Investors often borrow valuation approaches from industrial and financial businesses because lessors share characteristics with both.</p>
<p>Industrial companies may have large orderbooks and predictable earnings, strong free cash flow, lower leverage and substantial research costs. Financial companies often use more leverage and have less revenue visibility as loan books turn over.</p>
<p>Industrial analysts often focus on price-to-earnings, or P/E. Product economics, investment decisions, cost control and the conversion of earnings into cash can distinguish one company from another.</p>
<p>Financial analysts often focus on price-to-book, or P/B, alongside return on equity. Lending performance, hedging outcomes and well-timed acquisitions may create differences between firms.</p>
<p>AerCap argues that P/E can be particularly informative for aircraft leasing because long lease terms, constrained aircraft supply and relatively stable asset pricing can create substantial visibility over earnings and cash flows.</p>
<p>Price-to-book assumes book values are comparable across companies. Aircraft enter a lessor’s accounts at acquisition cost and are then depreciated; that carrying value can drift away from market value, especially with inflation.</p>
<p>There is generally no routine mark-to-market of the aircraft fleet between sales. A comparison based solely on reported book value can therefore miss the price and timing of earlier acquisitions.</p>
<p>The issue becomes more pronounced when aircraft are acquired through M&amp;A because transaction discounts and premiums can differ materially and may apply to very large portfolios.</p>
<p>Consider two leasing companies with identical fleets of 100 aircraft. Company A buys each aircraft for $40 million, giving a $4 billion book value. Company B pays $60 million each, giving a $6 billion book value.</p>
<p>The fleets are identical, but B’s book value is 50% higher because it paid more. If A is worth $4 billion, that is 1.0× its book; the same $4 billion value would be only about 0.67× B’s book.</p>
<p>Alternatively, if the equivalent fleets are each worth $6 billion, A trades at 1.5× book and B at 1.0×. Applying the same book multiple to both would hide the different acquisition prices.</p>
<p>Now suppose each fleet earns $600 million a year. At ten times earnings, both companies are worth $6 billion on P/E, despite the unequal book values.</p>
<p>Company A then earns a 15% return on its $4 billion equity book value, while B earns 10% on $6 billion. A higher price-to-book multiple for A can reflect stronger returns from buying the same assets more cheaply.</p>
<p>The lesson is to examine earnings, return on equity, purchase discipline and the fleet’s economic value together. AerCap presents P/E as a useful approach, not a guarantee that every lessor deserves the same earnings multiple.</p>
</div></div>

## Balance Sheet Discipline in Aircraft Leasing
<div class="aercap-beamer" data-aercap-beamer data-title="Balance Sheet Discipline in Aircraft Leasing"><div class="aercap-source">
<p>Aircraft lessors have significant financing requirements to fund and maintain large asset bases. They therefore need to manage interest-rate exposure, liquidity and other financial risks carefully.</p>
<p>AerCap describes balance-sheet discipline as a foundation of financial flexibility. It helps protect shareholders in volatile markets and leaves room to invest through different cycles.</p>
<p>The first element is leverage. At the time of the video, AerCap targeted net debt-to-equity of about 2.7×: enough debt to support attractive equity returns while maintaining protection for bondholders.</p>
<p>The second element is diversified funding. AerCap described investment-grade access to both unsecured and secured bond markets, plus more than 100 banking relationships globally.</p>
<p>Different lenders, markets and debt structures give the company alternatives when a particular funding channel becomes expensive or unavailable, and capacity to act when aircraft purchase opportunities arise.</p>
<p>AerCap also described maintaining liquidity at 1.5× the next twelve months’ sources-to-uses. The measure compares available sources of cash and funding with expected needs over that period.</p>
<p>The third element is asset-liability matching. Because lease rents are typically fixed at the outset and can run for six to twelve years, the lessor has substantial visibility over future cash flows.</p>
<p>Most of the associated debt is fixed rate too. When AerCap borrows at a floating rate, it can use hedges to limit how much changing rates alter its financing costs.</p>
<p>Matching the timing, duration and rate structure of leases and debt reduces duration risk: the risk that funding reprices or matures while lease income remains fixed. This helps preserve flexibility and long-run shareholder value.</p>
