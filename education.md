## Power-by-the-Hour
<div class="aercap-beamer" data-aercap-beamer data-title="Power-by-the-Hour"><div class="aercap-source">
<p>Today I’ll spend a few minutes explaining the impact of Power-by-the-Hour lease contracts, why we entered into them, as well as the impact on our revenues and cash flows.</p>
<p>So, what are Power-by-the-Hour contracts? Whilst the vast majority of leases that AerCap enters into are fixed rate from the outset, during the Covid years we offered a small selection of variable contracts where payments were based on the monthly utilisation of the aircraft, called Power-by-the-Hour, or PBH. After this initial PBH period, these leases would then revert to fixed-rate rentals for the remainder of the contract.</p>
<p>These contracts have always been part of the toolkit. Their usage increased significantly during Covid, peaking at around 140 contracts, or approximately 10%.</p>
<p>The reason for their popularity was that government restrictions meant travel corridors around the world were constantly reopening and closing. Even though airlines were confident in the long-term usage of the aircraft, the short term was likely to be more variable.</p>
<p>It was attractive to AerCap also, as it meant we protected the back-end lease rates rather than locking in low rates for longer. In practice, this worked well for both AerCap and the airlines as utilisation climbed steadily.</p>
<p>Now to the accounting. When a lease has a variable element, the total fixed lease revenues are averaged over the contract in what is known as straight-line rental.</p>
<p>In a simplified five-year example, assume the first year is calculated on a PBH basis and the lease then reverts to a fixed rental of $1 million per year. If there is no utilisation in year one, cash receipts are zero in year one and $1 million in each later year. The $4 million of fixed contract revenue is recognised at $800,000 per year on a straight-line basis.</p>
<p>Now assume the aircraft is utilised 65% of the time in year one. AerCap would collect $650,000 in PBH cash, recognised in that year, on top of the $800,000 straight-line rental. Accounting revenue in year one would therefore be $1.45 million, reducing to $800,000 in years two to five, while cash receipts rise from $650,000 in year one to $1 million per year thereafter.</p>
<p>AerCap referenced the PBH headwind in its 2024 guidance because many contracts were reverting to fixed-rate rentals. This was an accounting-revenue effect rather than a change in the underlying economics. Cash collections from those contracts were expected to increase as they moved to fully fixed-rate payments.</p>
</div></div>

## Aircraft Leasing 101
<div class="aercap-beamer" data-aercap-beamer data-title="Aircraft Leasing 101"><div class="aercap-source">
<p>Aircraft leasing has been around since the 1970s and has grown in importance and scale ever since. Aircraft lessors today own over 50% of the world’s commercial aircraft, and that share is expected to grow over coming decades.</p>
<p>Airlines use aircraft leasing as a source of financing and also to gain access to new-technology aircraft earlier than they might by going directly to manufacturers.</p>
<p>Aircraft leasing companies can contract manufacturer orderbooks years in advance. Earlier access to new aircraft can give airlines fuel-burn savings and significantly reduce their capital-expenditure needs.</p>
<p>Not all airlines operate in regions with deep capital markets. Leasing is therefore particularly important in countries with strong passenger growth but without the financial infrastructure required to fund large aircraft purchases directly.</p>
<p>So, what does a typical lease look like? For new aircraft, a common pattern is an initial twelve-year lease followed by two six-year leases or extensions, taking the aircraft towards a roughly 25-year useful life.</p>
<p>AerCap’s leases are almost exclusively fixed-rate in nature. For forward orders, there can be some variability in lease rates between signing the contract and delivery to account for changes in interest rates and inflation. Once the aircraft delivers, however, the rent is fixed for the lease term.</p>
<p>To match assets and liabilities, AerCap funds aircraft at group level using predominantly fixed-rate debt. Floating-rate debt can be hedged using interest-rate caps and swaps.</p>
<p>Combining long-term fixed-rate leases with long-term diversified fixed-rate financing aligns the capital structure and supports consistent returns through the cycle.</p>
</div></div>

## Maintenance Rights Assets
<div class="aercap-beamer" data-aercap-beamer data-title="Maintenance Rights Assets"><div class="aercap-source">
<p>Maintenance Rights Assets, or MRA, are assets created when aircraft with existing leases are acquired, usually through M&amp;A. They represent the right of the new owner to receive the aircraft back in an improved maintenance condition relative to its condition at the acquisition date.</p>
<p>The concept is particularly relevant to lessors that engage in large-scale M&amp;A because it is a feature of purchase accounting. AerCap encountered substantial MRA after the acquisition of ILFC in 2014 and GECAS in 2021.</p>
<p>Purchase-price accounting requires the value of an acquired leased aircraft to be split into two components. The first is flight equipment held for operating lease — essentially the metal value of the aircraft. The second is the value of the maintenance rights tied to the lease: the MRA.</p>
<p>Although the metal and the Maintenance Rights Asset are both part of the aircraft’s economic value and trade together on sale, they are treated differently for accounting purposes.</p>
<p>The metal value is depreciated on a straight-line basis over the remaining life of the aircraft. The MRA is amortised on an event-driven basis over the remaining life of the lease. Events can include lease end, aircraft sale or a maintenance shop visit.</p>
<p>Suppose a company acquires a ten-year-old aircraft for $50 million with an existing two-year lease. The redelivery condition in the lease is $15 million better than the aircraft’s actual maintenance condition at acquisition.</p>
<p>The company would separate the $50 million acquisition cost into a $35 million flight-equipment asset and a $15 million MRA. The $35 million metal value is depreciated over the aircraft’s remaining life, while the $15 million MRA is amortised when the relevant event occurs during the remaining lease.</p>
<p>Relative to buying an economically identical aircraft outright, purchase accounting can therefore produce higher effective depreciation, lower reported asset values and lower profits in the earlier years after an acquisition.</p>
</div></div>

## Maintenance Reserves
<div class="aercap-beamer" data-aercap-beamer data-title="Maintenance Reserves"><div class="aercap-source">
<p>Maintenance reserves form a critical part of the cash flows associated with leasing aircraft.</p>
<p>Once an aircraft is delivered, the lessee takes responsibility for staffing, insurance, operations and maintenance. Each lease also specifies the condition in which the aircraft must be returned at the end of the term.</p>
<p>There are two common ways to deal with maintenance condition. The first is supplemental rent, usually called maintenance reserves. The second is end-of-lease, or EOL, compensation for the difference between actual return condition and the condition required by the lease.</p>
<p>A maintenance-reserve payer makes cash payments each month in addition to base rent. The balance accumulates like a sinking fund and helps ensure funds are available for major maintenance events such as shop visits.</p>
<p>Maintenance-reserve payments are typically linked to utilisation, often flight hours or flight cycles. If an aircraft moves between operators between major maintenance events, this mechanism helps allocate the maintenance cost to the operators whose use consumed the maintenance life.</p>
<p>EOL structures do not require the same monthly reserve payments. At lease end the airline can either perform the maintenance needed to meet the contractual return condition or pay the lessor the agreed shortfall. These arrangements are typically offered to stronger credits.</p>
<p>AerCap describes its maintenance-reserve accounting using a first-in, first-out approach. In a simplified twelve-year lease, suppose a $10 million shop visit is expected near lease end and the lessee pays $1 million per year in maintenance reserves.</p>
<p>For the first ten years, the cash collected is matched by a maintenance liability. Once collections exceed the amount expected to be spent on the maintenance event, additional collections can become maintenance revenue and flow through equity.</p>
<p>If the $10 million maintenance event occurs, the lessee pays for the work and claims reimbursement, reducing both cash and the maintenance liability.</p>
<p>Under an EOL contract, cash compensation received because the aircraft is returned below the stipulated maintenance condition is recorded as maintenance revenue. If maintenance work is later carried out, that cost flows through leasing expenses.</p>
</div></div>

## The Lifecycle of an Aircraft
<div class="aercap-beamer" data-aercap-beamer data-title="The Lifecycle of an Aircraft"><div class="aercap-source">
<p>The aircraft lifecycle begins with portfolio strategy: deciding which aircraft to own. AerCap focuses on in-demand new-technology assets with broad operator bases and deep orderbooks, because those characteristics create more placement options.</p>
<p>Stage two is purchasing. Negotiations between an OEM and a lessor can begin three to five years before expected delivery. Pricing, specification, flexibility, delivery schedules and payment terms all matter.</p>
<p>Stage three is leasing. Once an order is placed with the OEM, the lessor engages with suitable operators, evaluates local demand, assesses airline credit and seeks the strongest credit-adjusted lease opportunities.</p>
<p>Lease terms are often agreed twelve to thirty-six months before delivery. The first lease on a new aircraft is commonly around twelve years and is typically fixed-rate, providing visibility over future cash flows.</p>
<p>Stage four is the mid-life decision. Around years nine to twelve, the lessor considers extending the existing lease, re-leasing the aircraft or selling it. Age, condition, leasing demand and the wider market environment feed into a relative-value assessment.</p>
<p>AerCap generally prefers to sell aircraft with a lease already attached rather than “naked”, because financial buyers value the contracted cash-flow stream.</p>
<p>Stage five is end of life. The cycle of lease, transition and re-lease can repeat several times across an aircraft’s roughly 25-year passenger-service life.</p>
<p>From around year twenty onward, options can include selling the whole aircraft, selling engines, parting the aircraft out or converting it to cargo use. Cargo conversion can extend useful life substantially beyond the normal passenger-service period.</p>
</div></div>

## Managing Credit Risk in Aircraft Leasing
<div class="aercap-beamer" data-aercap-beamer data-title="Managing Credit Risk in Aircraft Leasing"><div class="aercap-source">
<p>Airlines are cyclical businesses, so investors can naturally assume that airline credit exposure must be the dominant risk in aircraft leasing. AerCap’s argument is that credit costs are not a major driver of performance for a well-run lessor.</p>
<p>It puts this down to three things: having the right lease structures, having the right aircraft assets and having the right attitude to collections.</p>
<p>Long-term fixed-rate leases provide predictable contractual cash flows. New aircraft are often placed on twelve-year leases, with protections including maintenance reserves, security deposits and return conditions.</p>
<p>The second factor is asset quality. AerCap’s view is that it can be preferable to have a highly desirable aircraft leased to a weaker airline than a weak aircraft leased to a very strong airline, because in-demand aircraft can be remarketed more readily if a customer gets into difficulty.</p>
<p>New-technology aircraft with large operator bases, strong backlogs, liquid pricing and aftermarket support give the lessor more placement alternatives.</p>
<p>The third factor is collections. A well-written lease still requires monitoring: timely cash collection, oversight of aircraft technical condition and open dialogue with customers.</p>
<p>Diversification by airline and geography also matters because it reduces overexposure to any single lessee or region and gives the lessor broader market intelligence.</p>
<p>AerCap’s conclusion is not that credit risk disappears, but that disciplined lease structures, liquid assets, active collections and diversification can contain its effect on long-run performance.</p>
</div></div>

## Valuation Practices in Aircraft Leasing
<div class="aercap-beamer" data-aercap-beamer data-title="Valuation Practices in Aircraft Leasing"><div class="aercap-source">
<p>Aircraft leasing sits between industrial and financial businesses, so investors often bring valuation methods from both sectors.</p>
<p>Industrial analysts often focus on price-to-earnings, or P/E, because earnings can reflect product strength, investment decisions, cost control and cash conversion. Financial analysts often focus on price-to-book, or P/B, together with return on equity.</p>
<p>AerCap argues that P/E can be particularly informative for aircraft leasing because long lease terms, constrained aircraft supply and relatively stable asset pricing can create substantial visibility over earnings and cash flows.</p>
<p>Price-to-book assumes book values are comparable across companies. In practice, aircraft enter lessors’ balance sheets at different acquisition prices and depreciated costs, and those accounting values can diverge from market values over time.</p>
<p>The issue becomes more pronounced when aircraft are acquired through M&amp;A because transaction discounts and premiums can differ materially and may apply to very large portfolios.</p>
<p>Consider two leasing companies with identical fleets of 100 aircraft. Company A buys each aircraft for $40 million, giving a $4 billion book value. Company B pays $60 million each, giving a $6 billion book value.</p>
<p>The fleets are identical, yet Company B’s book value is 50% larger simply because it paid more. Applying the same price-to-book multiple to both companies would therefore ignore the economics of the acquisition price.</p>
<p>If both fleets generate the same $600 million of annual earnings and the market values each company at ten times earnings, each would have a $6 billion market capitalisation. That equates to 1.5 times book for Company A but 1.0 times book for Company B.</p>
<p>The lesson is that book value alone can hide important differences. Earnings, return on equity, acquisition discipline and the underlying economics of the fleet all matter when comparing aircraft lessors.</p>
</div></div>

## Balance Sheet Discipline in Aircraft Leasing
<div class="aercap-beamer" data-aercap-beamer data-title="Balance Sheet Discipline in Aircraft Leasing"><div class="aercap-source">
<p>Aircraft lessors have significant financing requirements to fund and maintain large asset bases. They therefore need to manage interest-rate exposure, liquidity and other financial risks carefully.</p>
<p>AerCap describes balance-sheet discipline as a foundation of financial flexibility: a strong balance sheet can preserve the ability to invest through different market cycles and protect the business during volatility.</p>
<p>The first element is leverage. AerCap targets net debt-to-equity of around 2.7 times, seeking a balance between equity returns and protection for bondholders.</p>
<p>The second element is funding sources and liquidity. Diversified access to secured and unsecured debt markets and a broad banking network reduces dependence on any single source of capital.</p>
<p>AerCap also describes maintaining liquidity at 1.5 times the next twelve months’ sources-to-uses, creating a buffer against upcoming funding needs.</p>
<p>The third element is asset-liability matching. Because lease rents are typically fixed at the outset and can run for six to twelve years, the lessor has substantial visibility over future cash flows.</p>
<p>Most debt is also fixed rate. Where borrowing is floating rate, hedges can be used to manage the exposure.</p>
<p>Aligning the duration and rate structure of the assets with the liabilities helps the lessor manage duration risk and reduce the chance that financing costs move sharply against fixed lease income.</p>
