This is a reconstructed HTML version of an earlier school CBA investigation into **how hot drinks cool over time**.

The original project combined experimental measurement with mathematical modelling. Rather than leaving the work in an old document, I have retained it here as part of the longer project timeline.

## The Question

How does the temperature of a hot drink change with time, and how can factors such as the **cup material** and whether the drink is **covered** affect the cooling curve?

The trials began at approximately the same starting temperature so that the results could be compared more meaningfully.

## Variables

- **Dependent variable:** temperature, measured in °C
- **Continuous independent variable:** time
- **Categorical variable:** cup material
- **Binary variable:** covered or uncovered

## The Mathematics

The important feature of the data is that cooling is **not linear**. A hot drink normally cools quickly at first and then more slowly as its temperature approaches the surrounding room temperature.

A standard model is

\[
T(t)=T_a+(T_0-T_a)e^{-kt}
\]

where:

- \(T(t)\) is the temperature after time \(t\),
- \(T_a\) is the ambient temperature,
- \(T_0\) is the initial temperature,
- \(k\) controls the cooling rate.

This gives the cooling curve an exponential shape rather than a straight line.

## What I Learned

The project was useful because the mathematics emerged from a very ordinary experiment. Measuring temperature was only the first step; the interesting part was deciding how to represent the data, compare trials and choose a mathematical model that made sense.

It is an early example of the approach I still like: **collect or find data, visualise it, model it, and then ask what the model actually tells us.**
