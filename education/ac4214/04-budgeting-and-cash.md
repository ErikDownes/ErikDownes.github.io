## Budgets | Planning, coordination and control

A <button data-ac-term="budget">budget</button> is a quantified plan for future income, expenditure and activity. <button data-ac-term="budgetary control">Budgetary control</button> uses the plan as a basis for monitoring and corrective action.

The lecture highlights benefits including:

- planning and focus on the future;
- coordination between departments;
- motivation and responsibility;
- control and authorisation;
- identifying cash shortages early;
- identifying surplus cash that can be invested;
- supporting loan applications and supplier relationships.

Criticisms include time and bureaucracy, short-term focus, rigid structures, gaming/motivation problems and insufficient focus on value creation or innovation.

## Master budget | The budgets are interdependent

The <button data-ac-term="master budget">master budget</button> brings separate plans together. The sales budget often starts the chain because expected sales drive production/purchases and ultimately cash.

The lecture distinguishes:

- <button data-ac-term="incremental budgeting">incremental budgeting</button> — adjust the previous budget;
- <button data-ac-term="zero-based budgeting">zero-based budgeting</button> — justify expenditure from zero;
- operating budgets — typically fixed for a period;
- <button data-ac-term="rolling budget">rolling budgets</button> — extend the horizon continuously.

## Cash timing | Revenue is not the same as receipt

The most important budgeting skill in the archive is timing.

If sales are partly cash and partly credit, the cash budget records **when customers pay**, not when the sale is recognised. Purchases work the same way: a purchase can create a payment now or later.

<div class="ac-worked">
<strong>Lecture sales-receipts pattern</strong>
<p>Sales are 20% cash and 80% credit. Credit sales are collected 25% in the following month and 75% one month later.</p>
<p>For July receipts, combine July cash sales + 25% of June credit sales + 75% of May credit sales.</p>
</div>

<div class="ac-mcq" data-id="bud-receipt" data-answer="1" data-explain="A cash budget follows cash timing. June credit collected in August belongs in August, not June.">
<strong>A credit sale is made in June but paid in August. In which month's cash budget does the receipt appear?</strong>
<div class="ac-choices">
<button class="ac-choice">June</button>
<button class="ac-choice">August</button>
<button class="ac-choice">Both June and August</button>
</div><p class="ac-feedback"></p>
</div>

## Purchases | Use the inventory equation

Several question sets require purchases to be derived from cost of sales and inventory.

<div class="ac-formula">
Opening inventory + Purchases − Closing inventory = Cost of sales

Therefore:

Purchases = Cost of sales + Closing inventory − Opening inventory
</div>

When closing inventory is defined as a percentage of the **following month's sales/cost**, calculate that target first. This is one reason cash-budget questions often supply an extra month beyond the budget period.

## Cash budget | Build it in a fixed order

A <button data-ac-term="cash budget">cash budget</button> should be constructed systematically:

1. cash receipts from sales;
2. other cash inflows — grants, loans, disposals;
3. cash payments for purchases;
4. payroll/commission timing;
5. operating expenses;
6. interest, tax, equipment and other capital payments;
7. net cash flow;
8. opening cash/bank balance;
9. closing cash/bank balance.

<div class="ac-caution"><strong>Depreciation trap:</strong> <button data-ac-term="depreciation">depreciation</button> is an accounting expense but not a cash payment. It does not go into a cash budget unless the question involves actual cash expenditure on the asset.</div>

## Worked structure | Springsteen / Holland / Lavin pattern

The tutorial and exam questions vary the names and figures but reuse the same skills:

- staggered cash and credit receipts;
- staggered inventory payments;
- grant receipts;
- bank-loan inflows and monthly interest;
- salary and commission timing;
- equipment purchase;
- operating expenses that rise or fall by a percentage;
- opening overdraft/cash position.

The correct response is not to memorise a template of numbers. It is to build a **timeline** for each cash flow and only then total the months.

<div class="ac-mcq" data-id="bud-dep" data-answer="2" data-explain="Depreciation is non-cash. The equipment purchase is a cash outflow; depreciation is excluded from the cash budget.">
<strong>A machine costing €48,000 is bought for cash and depreciated over four years. What goes into the cash budget in the purchase month?</strong>
<div class="ac-choices">
<button class="ac-choice">Only annual depreciation</button>
<button class="ac-choice">Both purchase price and depreciation</button>
<button class="ac-choice">The €48,000 purchase payment, not depreciation</button>

## Control | Why the bank cares

The 2024 exam explicitly asks whether a bank manager is more interested in cash flows or profits when reviewing a loan. Profit matters for performance, but debt service is paid in cash. A profitable company can still fail if its cash arrives too late.

That is the bridge to <button data-ac-term="liquidity">liquidity</button> and working capital.

<div class="ac-interview">
<strong>Interview carry-out</strong>
<p>“Budgeting is not just forecasting a profit. The cash-budget work taught me to model when money is actually collected and paid, which is critical for liquidity. I would map the timing assumptions first, then test whether the business needs short-term funding or has surplus cash.”</p>
</div>
