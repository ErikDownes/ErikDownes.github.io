## Full absorption cost | What does a unit cost when production overhead is included?

<button data-ac-term="full absorption cost">Full absorption cost</button> assigns a unit its direct production costs plus a share of production overhead.

A useful structure is:

<div class="ac-formula">
Direct materials  
+ Direct labour  
+ Direct expenses  
= Prime cost

Prime cost  
+ Absorbed production overhead  
= Full production cost
</div>

Production overhead cannot usually be traced directly to a single unit, so an **overhead absorption rate (OAR)** is applied using an activity base such as labour hours or machine hours.

<div class="ac-formula">
OAR = Budgeted production overhead ÷ Budgeted activity

Overhead absorbed by a job/unit = OAR × Activity used
</div>

<div class="ac-worked">
<strong>Worked example</strong>
<p>Budgeted factory overhead is €240,000 for 12,000 machine hours, so OAR = €20 per machine hour.</p>
<p>A job uses direct materials €1,600, direct labour €900 and 30 machine hours.</p>
<p>Absorbed overhead = 30 × €20 = €600. Full production cost = <strong>€3,100</strong>.</p>
</div>

## Full cost is not relevant cost

This distinction matters enormously.

A full-cost system is useful for inventory valuation, longer-term pricing/cost recovery and understanding resource consumption. A **decision** about a one-off order, make-or-buy choice or use of spare capacity needs <button data-ac-term="relevant cost">relevant costs</button> — future cash flows that differ.

Allocated fixed overhead can appear in the full cost while being irrelevant to the short-term decision.

<div class="ac-mcq" data-id="full-relevant" data-answer="2" data-explain="Full absorption costing allocates production overhead for product costing; relevant costing filters future cash flows that actually change between options.">
<strong>Why can full absorption cost and relevant cost give different numbers for the same product?</strong>
<div class="ac-choices">
<button class="ac-choice">Because relevant costing never includes materials</button>
<button class="ac-choice">Because full costing uses only cash flows</button>
<button class="ac-choice">Because full costing allocates overhead while decision costing includes only changing future cash flows</button>
</div><p class="ac-feedback"></p>
</div>

## Sources of finance | Match the funding to the need

Businesses can finance assets and operations using internal and external sources. The practical decision is not “which source is cheapest?” in isolation; it is which source fits the **amount, duration, risk, control and cash-flow profile**.

<div class="ac-grid">
<div class="ac-card">
<h3>Internal finance</h3>
<ul>
<li>retained earnings / internally generated cash;</li>
<li>release of working capital;</li>
<li>sale of non-essential assets.</li>
</ul>
<p>No new external claim is created, but internal cash has an opportunity cost.</p>
</div>
<div class="ac-card">
<h3>Short-term external finance</h3>
<ul>
<li>bank overdraft / revolving short-term facilities;</li>
<li>trade credit;</li>
<li>other short-term borrowing.</li>
</ul>
<p>Flexible for working-capital needs, but refinancing/rate risk matters.</p>
</div>
<div class="ac-card">
<h3>Long-term debt</h3>
<ul>
<li>term loans;</li>
<li>bonds/debentures in larger companies;</li>
<li>asset-backed borrowing.</li>
</ul>
<p>Preserves ownership but creates fixed interest/principal obligations and may require security/covenants.</p>
</div>
<div class="ac-card">
<h3>Equity and leasing</h3>
<ul>
<li>new share capital;</li>
<li>owner investment;</li>
<li>leasing/asset finance where appropriate.</li>
</ul>
<p>Equity has no compulsory interest payment but can dilute ownership; leasing can align funding with use of an asset.</p>
</div>
</div>

## The matching principle | Do not fund a long-lived asset with tomorrow's cash crisis

A basic financing principle is to match the duration of finance to the economic life and risk of the asset.

- seasonal inventory or a temporary receivable build-up may justify short-term finance;
- a factory, aircraft, major system or long-lived machine usually needs stable long-term funding;
- permanent working-capital requirements should not depend entirely on facilities that can disappear overnight.

## How to compare financing choices

Ask:

- **Cost:** interest, fees, expected equity return.
- **Cash-flow burden:** when must interest/principal/dividends be paid?
- **Risk:** refinancing, interest-rate, covenant and default risk.
- **Security:** are assets pledged?
- **Control:** does ownership or voting power change?
- **Flexibility:** can the finance expand, contract or be repaid early?
- **Maturity:** does the funding last as long as the need?
- **Balance-sheet impact:** leverage and future borrowing capacity.
- **Strategic fit:** does the financing preserve options?

<div class="ac-mcq" data-id="fin-match" data-answer="1" data-explain="Long-lived assets normally require stable long-term funding so the financing maturity is aligned with the asset's life and cash generation.">
<strong>Which financing choice best reflects basic maturity matching?</strong>
<div class="ac-choices">
<button class="ac-choice">Fund a 20-year asset entirely with an overnight overdraft</button>
<button class="ac-choice">Use stable long-term finance for a long-lived asset</button>
<button class="ac-choice">Use equity only for inventory lasting two weeks</button>
